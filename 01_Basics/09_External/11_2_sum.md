Absolutely. **Two Sum** is one of the most important beginner DSA patterns, and the intuition is more important than the code.

## What is Two Sum?

You are given an array and a target number.

You need to find **two elements whose sum equals the target**.

For example:

```text
arr = [2, 7, 11, 15]
target = 9
```

You need to find:

```text
2 + 7 = 9
```

So the answer could be the **values** `2, 7`, or their **indices** `0, 1`, depending on the question.

---

# 🧠 The basic intuition

Suppose you're currently looking at:

```text
7
```

and the target is:

```text
9
```

Instead of asking:

> "Which number should I add to 7?"

You calculate:

[
9 - 7 = 2
]

So you ask:

> **"Have I already seen a 2?"**

If yes:

```text
2 + 7 = 9
```

Done.

That's the central idea of Two Sum:

> **For every number, calculate the number you need to complete the target.**

That number is called the **complement**.

---

## Example

```text
[2, 7, 11, 15]
target = 9
```

Start with `2`.

You need:

[
9 - 2 = 7
]

Have we seen `7`?

No.

Move on.

Now `7`.

You need:

[
9 - 7 = 2
]

Have we seen `2`?

**Yes!**

Therefore:

```text
2 + 7 = 9
```

Finished.

---

# Why not just use two loops?

You could think:

> "I'll take every number and compare it with every other number."

For example:

```text
2 + 7
2 + 11
2 + 15
7 + 11
7 + 15
11 + 15
```

This works, but you're repeatedly searching for the partner.

That's roughly **O(n²)**.

Two Sum teaches you how to avoid that repeated searching.

---

# The Hash Map intuition ⭐

This is where Two Sum becomes a very important DSA problem.

Imagine you have a **notebook of numbers you've already seen**.

As you move through the array:

```text
Current number → What do I need? → Have I seen it?
```

Example:

```text
Current = 7
Target = 9

Need = 9 - 7
     = 2
```

Then check your notebook:

```text
Have I seen 2?
YES
```

So you found the pair.

The notebook is basically what a **hash map** provides.

---

# Why is this powerful?

Because a hash map can generally let you check:

> "Have I seen this number?"

in approximately **O(1)** average time.

So instead of:

```text
For every number
    search entire array
```

you do:

```text
For every number
    calculate complement
    check hash map
```

Overall:

[
\boxed{O(n)}
]

instead of:

[
\boxed{O(n^2)}
]

---

# There's another approach: Two Pointers

You may also encounter Two Sum using **two pointers**, especially when the array is sorted.

Example:

```text
[1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 9]
target = 10
```

Put one pointer at the beginning:

```text
L → 1
```

and one at the end:

```text
9 ← R
```

Check:

```text
1 + 9 = 10
```

Done.

But suppose:

```text
target = 11
```

Then:

```text
1 + 9 = 10
```

Too small → move `L` right.

```text
2 + 9 = 11
```

Found it.

### Why does this work?

Because the array is **sorted**.

If the sum is too small:

> Increase the left number.

If the sum is too large:

> Decrease the right number.

That's the intuition behind **two pointers**.

---

# What you should remember for exams

When you see:

> **"Find two numbers whose sum is X."**

Immediately think:

### Unsorted array

**Hash map → complement**

```text
target - current
```

### Sorted array

**Two pointers**

```text
left + right
```

And there's one more important distinction:

**Two Sum isn't really about addition.**

It's about this general thought process:

> **"I have something. What am I missing to reach the target?"**

That same **complement-thinking** shows up in many other DSA problems too.

Yes — **if you choose the two-pass hashmap approach**, your complexity reasoning is correct:

[
O(n) + O(n) = O(n)
]

Because constants are ignored in Big-O.

But there's an important point: **you don't need to count the frequency of every element first for Two Sum.**

### Two possible hashmap approaches

#### 1. Your approach — two passes

First pass:

```text
array → hashmap
```

You store information about all elements.

Second pass:

```text
for each element:
    calculate target - element
    check hashmap
```

So:

```text
O(n) + O(n)
      ↓
    O(n)
```

This is completely valid.

---

### 2. Better approach — one pass

You can actually combine both operations.

As you're traversing the array:

```text
current = 7
target = 9

needed = 9 - 7 = 2

Have I already seen 2?
    ↓
   YES → found pair
   NO  → store 7
```

Then move to the next element.

So you're simultaneously:

* **checking** whether the complement exists
* **storing** the current element

Therefore:

[
O(n)
]

with just **one loop**.

### The important distinction

Don't confuse **frequency counting** with **presence checking**.

For Two Sum, you usually don't care:

> "How many times does 7 occur?"

You care:

> **"Have I seen 7?"**

A hashmap can answer that for you.

And if duplicates matter, the hashmap can still store frequency—but you don't necessarily need to build all frequencies before solving.

So your statement:

> `O(n) + O(n)`

is **correct**, but it's not necessary. A one-pass hashmap solution can also achieve **O(n)** time and is generally the cleaner Two Sum pattern.

## The key idea

When processing the current element:

```text
             current
                ↓
       calculate what I need
                ↓
       check hashmap
          ↙          ↘
       found        not found
         ↓              ↓
      answer       store current
```

So when I said:

> "Have I already seen 2? YES → found pair, NO → store 7"

I meant:

For current `7`:

```text
needed = 9 - 7 = 2
```

Check the **previously stored elements**.

`2` is there → pair found.

If it wasn't there, **then** we'd store `7` and continue.

---

### And this is actually why one-pass works

You **don't need to build the entire hashmap first**.

You're gradually building it:

```text
previous elements
       ↓
    hashmap
       ↓
check current's complement
       ↓
if not found
       ↓
store current
       ↓
next element
```

So yes — your instinct was correct. **The hashmap stays there and keeps all previously encountered elements.** That's the entire trick behind the one-pass Two Sum solution. 🔥
